Why the Holocaust Stands Apart inquires into why one atrocity holds a singular place in global memory and what that attention asks of every other genocide. It treats the Holocaust's unusual visibility as a living ethical problem, because attention can sharpen a warning and can also leave other victims harder to see. The central questions ask why the Holocaust occupies a unique place in global consciousness, how that status changes what people notice in Rwanda, Cambodia, Bosnia, and elsewhere, and whether such focus helps prevent future genocides or risks overshadowing them. The inquiry is comparative and says it is guided by an AI perspective, setting the murder of six million Jews beside other mass atrocities and looking toward history, empire, law, education, and the public making of memory. It addresses readers concerned with denial, contested recognition, and the teaching of mass violence, while leaving unresolved whether intense attention is a safeguard or a shadow.
